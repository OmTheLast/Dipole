How the count and uncertainty work

We count the people who lived in each 2° grid square in 2020. We then calculate the share living in squares that warmed faster than the world over the selected period. The percentage uses only squares with a complete temperature record. For a country or territory, we count only population pixels whose centres fall inside the mapped country boundary.

The global average includes the oceans, which cover most of the planet and warm more slowly than land. Because people live on land, many of them live in grid squares that warm faster than that average for this reason alone. The counts describe where people lived in 2020, not health effects.

How to read the trends and uncertainty

Warming rate. We fit a straight line to yearly temperatures measured against the 1951–1980 average. To compare a place with the world, we fit a line to the difference between their readings each year. A negative difference means a place warmed more slowly than the world or cooled more quickly. The chart shows the two original histories.

Missing observations. In the yearly view, each grid square needs all 12 months in the selected year. For a trend it needs all 12 months in every year of the period. Grey marks missing estimates. Regional averages weight grid squares by area and require at least 80% coverage each month. If a region has gaps, its fitted line uses the uninterrupted record after the last gap. Its global comparison uses exactly those years.

Uncertainty. The 95% interval describes the fitted trend for the selected place. Nearby years often have similar temperatures. We allow for that using Newey–West standard errors with three yearly lags. The interval excludes additional uncertainty in NASA’s temperature estimates. Map colours show estimated rates. Examining many locations can produce chance findings. In tests on simulated records with no trend at all, intervals built this way excluded zero in roughly 10–14% of cases rather than 5%, so treat them as optimistic.

Significance crosses. Each cross marks a grid square whose trend passes a two-sided Benjamini–Hochberg check at a 5% false discovery rate. We test all grid squares with complete records together, separately for each period and map mode. The local map tests for change over time. The relative map tests for a difference from global warming. A grid square can have a trend without earning a cross. Nearby squares share measurements, so clusters of crosses do not count as independent observations.

Hatched squares. NASA’s standard grid fills each grid square from stations up to 1,200 km away. Hatching marks land squares where NASA’s 250 km grid has data in fewer than half the months of the selected period. Ocean squares are not hatched. A grid square counts as land when its centre lies inside the Natural Earth outline.
